\title{FedMATRIX-LoRA: Client-Aware Matrix-Level Adaptation for Resource-Constrained Federated Fine-Tuning}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Federated fine-tuning must allocate limited training resources across clients with heterogeneous data and device capabilities. For low-rank adaptation (LoRA), this requires deciding not only adapter capacity but also which target projections receive updates. Grouping projections into indivisible Transformer blocks restricts this placement decision. A controlled study using client-local importance scores shows larger short-term validation-loss reductions with independent matrix selection than with whole-layer allocation across six partition--checkpoint settings. Motivated by this observation, we present FedMATRIX-LoRA, a client-aware matrix-level adaptation framework. Clients estimate update priorities from local gradient profiles and construct resource-feasible matrix configurations; the server coordinates participation and configuration choice under a shared budget and latency objective. Matrix-wise aggregation combines the resulting heterogeneous updates. A conditional stationarity analysis characterizes the roles of update bias and matrix coverage. Experiments spanning image classification, language understanding, and dialogue summarization evaluate the complete framework, while controlled studies examine selection granularity, scoring reliability, and the contributions of individual components. The results support importance-guided matrix selection as a useful adaptation dimension under constrained training budgets.

\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Federated fine-tuning, low-rank adaptation, client-aware matrix selection, resource heterogeneity
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Foundation models provide reusable representations, but adapting them to downstream applications often requires data held by different institutions or devices. Federated learning enables these data holders to optimize a shared model without pooling their raw examples~\cite{mcmahan2017communication,kairouz2021advances}. Full-parameter updates remain costly for clients with limited memory, computation, and communication capacity. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) reduces this burden by representing updates through compact trainable factors while keeping the pretrained backbone fixed~\cite{hu2022lora}. Federated LoRA therefore makes collaborative adaptation more accessible, but still requires deciding which adapter parameters each client should update with its available resources.

Adapter size alone does not determine training feasibility. Memory is also required for the frozen backbone, optimizer states, and intermediate activations retained for backpropagation. Reducing rank shrinks adapter-related state without necessarily shortening the backward path to a trainable location. Fed-HeLLo identifies this distinction and reports limited total-memory savings from rank reduction when activations dominate~\cite{zhang2025fedhello}. Selecting trainable locations thus complements rank adaptation: it changes where updates occur and, under partial-adapter execution, which activation dependencies must be retained. We study this placement decision at a fixed rank, separating target selection from capacity allocation within each selected adapter.

A Transformer block contains distinct target projections, including Query, Key, Value, and feed-forward transformations. Each projection can receive its own LoRA update. Existing placement studies and importance-aware rank allocation show that adaptation value need not be uniform across matrices or layers~\cite{hu2022lora,fm_adalora2023}. Layer-level allocation nevertheless binds candidate projections within a block into one decision. Under a limited budget, selecting a useful projection can therefore require allocating capacity to other projections in the same block, while excluding useful targets elsewhere. This motivates a more specific question than whether different locations matter: with the importance criterion and parameter budget held fixed, does relaxing whole-layer binding improve the configurations selected for adaptation?

To examine this question, we compare matrix-level and whole-layer allocation under identical client-local importance scores and matched training budgets. Using CIFAR100/ViT-base, the matrix-level scheme independently selects 12 Q/K/V projections, whereas the layer-level scheme selects all three projections in four Transformer blocks. We evaluate IID and two non-IID partitions at initialization and after a shared warm-up, using paired short-term training runs. Section~\ref{sec:fm-granularity-study} describes the controlled protocol.

Figure~\ref{fig:fm-motivation} shows the additional held-out validation-loss reduction achieved by matrix-level selection. All six partition--checkpoint means are positive, and matrix-level selection outperforms whole-layer allocation in all 18 seed-level paired means. These observations motivate independent target-projection selection under a fixed parameter budget, rather than treating all projections within a Transformer block as an indivisible unit.

Exploiting this flexibility requires an assessment of candidate updates before the training budget is committed. Gradient- and Fisher-based criteria offer established ways to prioritize trainable parameters and LoRA layers~\cite{fm_fish2021,zhang2025fedhello}. Such priorities depend on both the current model and the data used to evaluate its loss. In a federated system, each client already holds data defining its local objective. Evaluating that objective provides a client-conditioned assessment without requiring an additional server-held profiling dataset. Fed-pilot also incorporates local and aggregated importance information into LoRA allocation~\cite{fm_fedpilot2025}; our focus is to expose individual projection choices within blocks and coordinate these choices under coupled resource constraints. Local profiling is the assessment mechanism, while the motivating comparison isolates the value of selection granularity.

A high-scoring subset, however, is useful only if it can be trained. Memory feasibility depends on the locations of selected targets as well as their number, and client computation and bandwidth determine whether the configuration meets a round deadline. Independently feasible choices can also exceed the shared round budget when executed together. The design therefore has two connected requirements: estimate update priorities at the target-matrix level, and translate those priorities into feasible client configurations that can be coordinated in a training round. A ranking alone does not resolve this second requirement.

We address these requirements with FedMATRIX-LoRA, a client-aware matrix-level adaptation framework. During profiling refreshes, each client evaluates the LoRA factors of candidate projections on a local mini-batch at the current global adapter. The resulting scores guide configurations at different matrix-equivalent costs, subject to local memory and latency constraints. The server selects client configurations under the shared budget and synchronization-latency objective. Clients train the assigned factors, and matrix-wise aggregation updates the shared global adapter. Local evidence informs the preferred targets; resource-aware allocation determines which proposed updates can be executed together.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
\item We formulate importance-guided target-matrix selection for resource-constrained federated fine-tuning, allowing projections within a Transformer block to be selected independently. A matched-budget study with client-local scoring demonstrates the benefit of independently selecting target projections over whole-layer allocation.
\item We develop FedMATRIX-LoRA to connect local matrix profiling with resource-feasible configuration generation, shared-budget coordination, and matrix-wise aggregation. Clients can update distinct target subsets while contributing to a common global adapter.
\item We provide a conditional stationarity analysis of selective aggregation and evaluate the framework across image classification, language understanding, and dialogue summarization. Controlled scoring and ablation studies examine the mechanisms separately from the complete system's performance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}\label{Sec.relatedworks}
\noindent\textbf{Parameter-efficient and importance-guided adaptation.}
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning reduces the parameters updated when adapting a pretrained model. LoRA expresses updates through low-rank factors attached to selected weight matrices, while QLoRA combines a quantized frozen backbone with trainable adapters~\cite{hu2022lora,dettmers2023qlora}. These mechanisms address different components of the adaptation footprint. Selecting the target subset introduces a further decision about where the trainable capacity is placed, rather than only its size or storage precision.

Importance-aware adaptation has already established that a limited parameter budget need not be distributed uniformly. AdaLoRA assigns nonuniform rank budgets across weight matrices through importance-guided pruning of an SVD-style update parameterization~\cite{fm_adalora2023}. FISH selects a sparse trainable-parameter mask using Fisher-based scores~\cite{fm_fish2021}. FedMATRIX-LoRA builds on importance-guided selection at a different allocation level: it chooses fixed-rank target subsets for individual federated clients. Whereas importance-aware rank allocation controls capacity within weight updates, the proposed formulation coordinates independently selectable target projections under heterogeneous client and round-level constraints.

\noindent\textbf{Heterogeneous federated LoRA allocation.}
HetLoRA supports different client ranks through local rank self-pruning and sparsity-weighted aggregation, while FAH-QLoRA jointly considers quantization and LoRA rank~\cite{cho2024hetlora,gao2025fah}. These approaches adapt capacity or numerical precision to client resources. Fed-HeLLo identifies an additional memory concern: reducing rank primarily affects optimizer-related state in the activation-dominated settings it studies, motivating partial-layer training~\cite{zhang2025fedhello}. LEGEND jointly configures tuning depth and rank distribution, explicitly linking trainable locations to resource use~\cite{liu2025legend}. Target selection and rank allocation are consequently complementary dimensions, rather than mutually exclusive alternatives.

Fed-HeLLo accounts for unequal layer importance through server-proxy scores and geometric allocation strategies~\cite{zhang2025fedhello}. Its score-guided strategy converts a shared assessment into allocation probabilities, so clients can receive different layer masks. FedMATRIX-LoRA instead permits separate choices among projections within a block and evaluates the scoring criterion on client data. The controlled study in Fig.~\ref{fig:fm-motivation} compares matrix-level and whole-layer selection using identical client-local scores and matched parameter budgets. It isolates the allocation-granularity decision rather than reproducing the full Fed-HeLLo framework.

Fed-pilot is a closely related importance-guided allocation method. Its block-indexed formulation combines local and aggregated gradient-based information-gain scores with memory-constrained allocation and compensated aggregation~\cite{fm_fedpilot2025}. FedMATRIX-LoRA exposes individual target-projection indicators within blocks and coordinates their configurations across clients under memory limits, latency constraints, and a shared round budget. The relevant distinction is the allocation decision and its coupled constraints, not local scoring or knapsack optimization alone.

\noindent\textbf{Aggregation and resource coordination.}
FedEx-LoRA addresses the mismatch associated with aggregating low-rank factors, and LoRA-FAIR refines aggregation and initialization~\cite{singhal2025fedex,bian2025lorafair}. These studies concern how local updates are combined. Oort, FedBalancer, and PyramidFL address participant selection or training-work coordination under heterogeneous conditions~\cite{lai2021oort,shin2022fedbalancer,li2022pyramidfl}. FedMATRIX-LoRA includes the trainable target subset in the configuration assigned to a participating client. Configuration generation connects local update priorities to resource feasibility, while server coordination determines which configurations can execute together. The matrix-wise convergence analysis characterizes the selective update pattern produced by this process.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}\label{sec.model}

\subsection{System Model and Federated LoRA Preliminaries}
We consider a heterogeneous federated LoRA fine-tuning system consisting of one server and $N$ clients. Each client $i$ owns a private local dataset $\mathcal{D}_i$, an on-device training-memory capacity $M_i^{\max}$ (corresponding to the available GPU device memory in our implementation), an estimated communication bandwidth $B_i$, and heterogeneous computational resources. To preserve data privacy, all local datasets remain on their corresponding clients throughout the training process and are never uploaded to the server. The server initializes a pretrained backbone model $\Theta^0$, which remains frozen during federated fine-tuning, and maintains the global LoRA adapter parameters $\theta^k$ at communication round $k$.

Let $n_i=|\mathcal{D}_i|$ denote the number of local  training samples at client $i$. Federated LoRA fine-tuning aims to minimize the following global objective
\begin{equation}
F(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_i(\theta),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
F_i(\theta)=
\frac{1}{n_i}
\sum_{z\in\mathcal{D}_i}
\ell(\Theta^0,\theta;z),
\label{eq:fl-objective}
\end{equation}
and $\ell(\cdot)$ denotes the task loss evaluated using the frozen backbone $\Theta^0$ and trainable LoRA parameters $\theta$.

For a target linear transformation $W_j\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, LoRA parameterizes its update as
\begin{equation}
\Delta W_j=B_jA_j,
\end{equation}
where $A_j\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ and $B_j\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}$ are low-rank matrices with rank $r\ll \min(d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}})$.

We treat the trainable adapter subset as a client-specific decision. A target matrix denotes a pretrained projection, such as Query, Key, or Value; selecting it means updating its associated LoRA factors, not the frozen pretrained matrix itself. The rank of every selected adapter is fixed to the same value, so configuration differences arise from target selection.

\subsection{Client-Aware Matrix-Level LoRA Allocation}
We formulate federated LoRA allocation at the target-matrix granularity.
Let
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}=\{W_1,W_2,\ldots,W_L\}
\end{equation}
denote the ordered set of LoRA-compatible target matrices extracted from the
backbone. These matrices include the Query, Key, Value, output, and feed-forward projection matrices in Transformer blocks. 

For client $i$ at communication round $k$, the matrix activation
decision is represented by the binary vector
\begin{eqnarray}
\mathbf m^k_i=[{m}_{i,1}^k,\cdots,{m}_{i,L}^k],
\end{eqnarray}
where
\begin{eqnarray}
&&{m}_{i,j}^k= \left\{
\begin{aligned}
&1,\mbox{if $W_j$ is activated for local training};\\
&0,\mbox{otherwise.}
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{eqnarray}Each activation vector therefore specifies the subset of LoRA target matrices trained by client $i$ during communication round $k$.

Let $V_i^k(\mathbf m_i^k)$ denote the profiled utility of client $i$'s matrix configuration. It is computed from local data at the current global adapter and summarizes the estimated priority of the selected updates. We set $V_i^k(\mathbf 0)=0$. The profiling procedure is defined in Section~\ref{sec:fm-profiling}.

Layer-group allocation is recovered by constraining all candidate matrices in a Transformer block to share the same selection indicator.

\subsection{Resource Constraints and Problem Formulation}
The activation decision of LoRA target matrices determines the resource consumption of each participating client. We model the training cost, training-memory usage, and training latency separately.

First, let $c_i(\mathbf m^k_i)$ denote the normalized training cost associated with matrix configuration $\mathbf m^k_i$. We approximate this cost by the number of activated rank-8 matrices, $c_i(\mathbf m^k_i)=\sum_{j=1}^{L}m_{i,j}^k$. This standardized count is not a measurement of matrix-specific computation or memory, which can differ even at the same rank; memory is modeled separately below.


Training-memory consumption depends on both the number and positions of activated matrices. For any nonzero \(\mathbf m\), let
\(\mathcal{J}(\mathbf m)=\{j\mid m_j=1\}\) and \(j_{\min}(\mathbf m)=\min\mathcal{J}(\mathbf m)\).
Following the training-memory decomposition commonly adopted in partial-layer federated LoRA fine-tuning \cite{zhang2025fedhello}, we set \(M_i(\mathbf0)=M_i^{\mathrm{fixed}}\), while a nonzero configuration uses
\begin{equation}
M_i(\mathbf m)=M_i^{\mathrm{fixed}}+q_i(r)c_i(\mathbf m)+\sum_{j=j_{\min}(\mathbf m)}^{L}\mu_{i,j},
\label{eq:memory}
\end{equation}
where
$M_i^{\mathrm{fixed}}$
denotes the memory occupied by the frozen backbone and fixed runtime buffers,
$q_i(r)$ is the parameter-and-optimizer memory per activated rank-\(r\) LoRA matrix, and
$\mu_{i,j}$ is retained activation memory. The last term reflects the position-dependent memory characteristic of Transformer
fine-tuning: activating an earlier matrix requires retaining a longer backward propagation path and therefore consumes more activation memory. 

The latency of client $i$ consists of local computation and communication. Let $\gamma_i$ denote its computation coefficient, $\sigma_i$ the communication size per activated matrix, and $B_i$ its available bandwidth. Its latency is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&&T_i^{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbf m_i)=\gamma_i c_i(\mathbf m_i),\\
&&T_i^{\mathrm{comm}}(\mathbf m_i)=\frac{\sigma_i c_i(\mathbf m_i)}{B_i},\\
&&T_i(\mathbf m_i)=T_i^{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbf m_i)+T_i^{\mathrm{comm}}(\mathbf m_i).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:latency-model}
\end{equation}
The coefficients define an analytical resource profile; equal matrix-equivalent costs need not correspond to identical measured hardware costs.


At communication round $k$, the server jointly determines the participating clients and their matrix configurations under a shared resource budget. Let $S_k$ denote the set of candidate clients,
$z_i^k\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether client $i$ participates in the current round,
$C^k$ denote the round-level training budget, and $\tau^k$ represent the latency of the current communication round. Let \(\ell_i^{\min}\) and \(\ell_i^{\max}\) bound the number of matrices activated by participating client \(i\), and let \(\lambda>0\) control the utility--latency trade-off.

\begin{align}
\max_{\{\mathbf m_i^k,z_i^k\},\tau^k}
\quad&
\sum_{i\in S_k}
V_i^k(\mathbf m_i^k)
-\lambda\tau^k
\label{eq:joint-objective}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&
\sum_{i\in S_k}
c_i(\mathbf m_i^k)
\leq C^k,
\label{eq:joint-budget}\\
&
m_{i,j}^k\leq z_i^k,
\label{eq:participation}\\
&
\ell_i^{\min}z_i^k
\leq
\sum_{j=1}^{L}m_{i,j}^k
\leq
\ell_i^{\max}z_i^k,
\label{eq:bound}\\
&
M_i(\mathbf m_i^k)
\leq M_i^{\max},
\quad
\forall i:z_i^k=1,
\label{eq:joint-memory}\\
&
T_i(\mathbf m_i^k)
\leq
\tau^k
\leq
T_{\max},
\quad
\forall i:z_i^k=1 .
\label{eq:joint-latency}
\end{align}
The objective maximizes the aggregated client-conditioned adaptation utility while minimizing round latency. Equations~\eqref{eq:joint-budget}--\eqref{eq:joint-latency} impose the round-level matrix-equivalent budget, participation consistency, activated-matrix bounds, per-client training-memory limits, and the round deadline. In particular, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:participation} and \eqref{eq:bound} together ensure that an inactive client selects no matrix, whereas an active client selects between \(\ell_i^{\min}\) and \(\ell_i^{\max}\) matrices.


The formulation couples client-level feasibility with a shared round budget. We implement it by constructing feasible configurations locally and coordinating these configurations at the server.

\section{FedMATRIX-LoRA Design}\label{Sec.alg}
\subsection{Framework Overview}
FedMATRIX-LoRA implements importance-guided matrix selection through client-conditioned assessment and resource-feasible allocation. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} separates these roles. Clients profile the current adapter on local data and form configurations of independently selectable targets. The server coordinates the configurations under the round budget and latency objective. Clients then update the assigned factors, and their contributions are aggregated matrix-wise into the next shared adapter. The following subsections specify the scoring rule, allocation procedure, training protocol, and conditional analysis.

\subsection{Client-Conditioned Matrix Profiling}\label{sec:fm-profiling}
\noindent\textbf{Client-conditioned assessment.}
The motivating study isolates the value of allowing individual projections to be selected. To use that flexibility during federated training, the allocation procedure needs a priority score for each candidate target. At a common global adapter, a client's loss gradient reflects parameter sensitivity under its own examples. Local assessment therefore supplies priorities associated with that client's objective without requiring a server-held profiling dataset. The configuration generator combines these priorities with resource constraints rather than treating an isolated ranking as the final training decision.

\noindent\textbf{A single-pass gradient score.}
Gradient- and Fisher-based criteria have been used to select trainable parameters and LoRA layers~\cite{fm_fish2021,zhang2025fedhello}. We use a gradient-squared criterion that evaluates all candidate targets in one backward pass, rather than trial-training individual configurations. During a refresh at round $k$, client $i$ attaches LoRA modules to the candidate projections, loads the current global adapter $\theta^k$, and evaluates the average loss on a local mini-batch $\mathcal B_i^k$. Let $\theta_j^k$ denote the LoRA factors associated with target matrix $j$. Its raw score is
\begin{equation}
 g_{i,j}^k=\sum_{p\in\theta_j^k}
 \left(\nabla_p\mathcal L_i(\theta^k;\mathcal B_i^k)\right)^2.
 \label{eq:fim}
\end{equation}
The score aggregates the magnitude of the batch-loss gradient over the adapter's trainable coordinates. Squaring makes coordinate contributions nonnegative; summation yields a block-level sensitivity measure rather than a mean per parameter. For a fixed parameterization, the gradient norm measures the largest first-order sensitivity to a unit-norm perturbation of that block. This motivates using its square as an update-priority signal. The calculation uses the gradient of a batch-averaged loss, so the score is a Fisher-inspired proxy rather than exact Fisher information. Its empirical relation to short-term training gains is evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:fm-score-study}.

Both the local examples and the current adapter enter the score. Clients can therefore assign different priorities to a common target, and refreshes allow those priorities to evolve with the global model. Each profiling pass evaluates the candidate set, including targets not updated by that client in the preceding round. The next allocation is consequently informed by more than the client's previously active subset. The single-pass design supplies this information without a separate optimization run for every candidate configuration.

\noindent\textbf{Shared positional calibration.}
The raw scores are supplemented by a structural preference over candidate positions. Prior partial-LoRA work reports favorable performance--resource trade-offs for deeper adaptation in its studied settings~\cite{liu2025legend}. We implement a multiplicative factor:
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde g_{i,j}^k=g_{i,j}^k(1+d_j)^{\rho},
 \label{eq:depth}
\end{equation}
where $d_j=(j-1)/(L-1)$ is the normalized position in the ordered candidate set. The evaluated schedule is $\rho=1+1.5\mathbb I_{\mathrm{pair}}+\eta$, where $\mathbb I_{\mathrm{pair}}$ indicates a sentence-pair task and $\eta=0,1,2$ corresponds to IID, ordinary non-IID, and severe non-IID settings. The schedule is fixed by the configuration, while the client-dependent component remains $g_{i,j}^k$.

The common multiplier can change a client's ranking but does not replace its data-dependent evidence or enforce a common mask. It is an auxiliary preference, distinct from the decision to permit individual target selection. The controlled granularity study holds it fixed within every matrix--layer pair. Section~\ref{sec:fm-ablations} separately evaluates the end-to-end effect of removing it.

\noindent\textbf{Configuration utility and refresh.}
Server coordination compares configurations proposed by different clients. The calibrated scores therefore use a common normalization scale:
\begin{equation}
 G_{\max}^k=\max_{\substack{h\in S_k\\1\le j\le L}}\widetilde g_{h,j}^k,
 \qquad v_{i,j}^k=
 \frac{\widetilde g_{i,j}^k}{\max\{G_{\max}^k,\delta\}}\in[0,1],
 \label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
where $\delta>0$ prevents division by zero. A common divisor retains relative calibrated magnitudes across clients and matrices and sets the utility scale in the server's utility--latency objective. The assignment utility is
$V_i^k(\mathbf m_i^k)=\sum_{j=1}^{L}m_{i,j}^kv_{i,j}^k$.
This additive score supplies a tractable selection objective; it does not require the full training loss to decompose into independent matrix losses. Local evidence, the positional preference, and the common scale have separate roles in constructing that objective.

Profiles are cached between designated refresh rounds and recomputed when missing or due for refresh. Reuse avoids an additional backward pass at every allocation decision; refreshing reconnects the scores to the current adapter. Raw examples and profiling gradients remain local, while coordination uses matrix scores and configuration summaries. Section~\ref{sec:fm-score-study} evaluates the ranking information supplied by the score, and Section~\ref{sec:fm-ablations} distinguishes scoring-source, granularity, and calibration effects within the complete training pipeline.

\subsection{Resource-Feasible Matrix Allocation}\label{sec:fm-allocation}
For a fixed round \(k\), superscripts are omitted from configuration attributes and scheduling variables when unambiguous. Each feasible configuration \(\kappa\) generated by client \(i\) is represented as
\[
\kappa=(V_{i\kappa},c_{i\kappa},T_{i\kappa},M_{i\kappa},\mathbf m_{i\kappa}),
\]
where \(V_{i\kappa}\), \(c_{i\kappa}\), \(T_{i\kappa}\), \(M_{i\kappa}\), and \(\mathbf m_{i\kappa}\) denote the profiled adaptation utility, normalized matrix-equivalent cost, estimated latency, estimated training-memory consumption, and binary target-matrix assignment, respectively. Each client uploads a compact configuration menu \(\mathcal K_i^k\).

\noindent\textbf{Local configurations.}
Under the adopted memory model, feasibility depends on the selected-matrix count and the earliest selected position through the retained-activation term. For each count $c$, the client therefore searches over feasible earliest positions and retains a configuration with the largest profiled utility. Let $Q_{i,e}^k(t)$ denote the maximum utility of selecting $t$ matrices from the suffix $\{e,\ldots,L\}$. Its recursion and the utility for configurations whose earliest selected position is $e$ are:
\(Q_{i,e}^k(0)=0\) and \(Q_{i,L+1}^k(t>0)=-\infty\).
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 Q_{i,e}^k(t)&=\max\left\{Q_{i,e+1}^k(t),v_{i,e}^k+Q_{i,e+1}^k(t-1)\right\},\quad t\ge1,
 \\
 \Phi_{i,e}^k(c)&=v_{i,e}^k+Q_{i,e+1}^k(c-1).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:client-dp}
\end{equation}

Only configurations satisfying the matrix-count, memory, and latency constraints are retained. Under the current count-based latency model, configurations with the same cost consume the same server budget and have the same latency; retaining the highest-utility feasible configuration at each cost therefore suffices for subsequent coordination.

\noindent\textbf{Server coordination.}
The server enumerates the distinct feasible latency thresholds $\tau\le T_{\max}$. For each threshold, it removes configurations exceeding $\tau$ and solves the remaining multiple-choice knapsack problem, choosing at most one configuration per client. With $H_i(w)$ denoting the largest utility attainable from the first $i$ client menus at budget $w$, the recursion is:

\begin{equation}
 H_i(w)=
 \max_{\kappa\in
 \mathcal K_i^k(\tau)\cup\{\varnothing\}:c_{i\kappa}\le w}
 \left\{
 H_{i-1}(w-c_{i\kappa})
 +
 V_{i\kappa}
 \right\},
 \label{eq:server-dp}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal K_i^k(\tau)$ is the menu after latency filtering, $\varnothing$ is the zero-utility inactive choice with zero cost, and $H_0(w)=0$.

The server selects the threshold and configuration assignment with the largest total utility minus $\lambda\tau$. This procedure optimizes the stated profiled objective; its role is to realize client-conditioned selection under the resource model. Algorithm~\ref{alg:fedmatrix} summarizes the complete training round.

\subsection{Local Training and Matrix-Wise Aggregation}

After determining the optimal configuration assignment, the server
dispatches the global adapter \(\theta^k\) together with the selected matrix
assignments to corresponding clients. Only selected LoRA parameters are updated locally. Task-specific trainable modules, if
required by the downstream task, are optimized jointly with the selected LoRA
parameters.

Since different clients may update different subsets of LoRA matrices, the
aggregation process is performed independently for each trainable matrix.
Let \(\mathcal U_j^k\) denote the clients returning updates for matrix \(j\), and let \(\theta_{i,j}^{k+1}\) be the corresponding locally updated LoRA parameters from client \(i\). The server performs matrix-wise client-uniform aggregation:

\begin{equation}
 \theta_j^{k+1}
 =
 \begin{cases}
 |\mathcal U_j^k|^{-1}
 \displaystyle\sum_{i\in \mathcal U_j^k}
 \theta_{i,j}^{k+1},
 &
 |\mathcal U_j^k|>0,
 \\[6pt]
 \theta_j^k,
 &
 |\mathcal U_j^k|=0 .
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:aggregation}
\end{equation}

The updated global adapter is used to initialize the next round. Because the updating client set can differ across target matrices, the aggregation averages only the clients contributing to each matrix and preserves the previous value when no update is available.

\subsection{Convergence of Matrix-Wise Aggregation}
\label{sec:fm-convergence}
We analyze the effective update induced by the matrix-wise aggregation rule. For this analysis, $\theta$ includes the LoRA parameters and any jointly trained shared task modules. Let $P^k$ project onto the parameter blocks selected for update in round $k$, as determined before local training. For a common positive round scale $\gamma$, define the actual aggregated direction by $v^k=(\theta^k-\theta^{k+1})/\gamma$. Conditioning on the current model, profiling information, and selected configuration, denoted by $\mathcal H_k$, write
\begin{align}
 b^k&=\mathbb E[v^k\mid\mathcal H_k]-P^k\nabla F(\theta^k),\\
 \xi^k&=v^k-\mathbb E[v^k\mid\mathcal H_k].
\end{align}
Thus $\mathbb E[\xi^k\mid\mathcal H_k]=0$, and the update is
\begin{equation}
 \theta^{k+1}=\theta^k-\gamma\bigl(P^k\nabla F(\theta^k)+b^k+\xi^k\bigr).
\end{equation}
The bias $b^k$ accounts for selective aggregation and the difference between local optimizer updates and the selected global gradient. This representation does not replace the experimental AdamW optimizer with SGD.

\noindent\textbf{Assumptions.}
Assume that $F$ is $\beta$-smooth on the relevant update segments and bounded below by $F_{\inf}$. Suppose the effective update errors satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E\|b^k\|^2\le B_{\mathrm{upd}}^2,\qquad
 \mathbb E\|\xi^k\|^2\le S_{\mathrm{upd}}^2.
\end{equation}
We additionally assume effective matrix coverage: for some $0<\alpha_{\mathrm{cov}}\le1$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cov},k}\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E\|P^k\nabla F(\theta^k)\|^2
 \ge\alpha_{\mathrm{cov}}\mathbb E\|\nabla F(\theta^k)\|^2
       -\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cov},k}.
\end{equation}
This condition quantifies the global gradient energy captured by the selected blocks.

\begin{theorem}[Expected stationarity]
\label{thm:fm-stationarity}
Under the stated assumptions, for $0<\gamma\le1/\beta$ and $K$ rounds,
\begin{align}
 \frac1K\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb E\|\nabla F(\theta^k)\|^2
 \le{}&\frac{2D_0}{\alpha_{\mathrm{cov}}\gamma K}\\
 &+\frac{B_{\mathrm{upd}}^2+\beta\gamma S_{\mathrm{upd}}^2
              +\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cov}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{cov}}},\nonumber
\end{align}
where $D_0=F(\theta^0)-F_{\inf}$ for a fixed initialization and
$\bar\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cov}}=K^{-1}\sum_k\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cov},k}$.
\end{theorem}
For a fixed horizon, setting $\gamma=c/\sqrt K\le1/\beta$ yields an $O(K^{-1/2})$ optimization and noise term when the error bounds and coverage constant are uniform across the analyzed runs. The residual is governed by effective-update bias and uncovered gradient energy. The result characterizes average stationarity, rather than optimal test accuracy.

The contribution of client selection can be made explicit. Let $n_j=|\mathcal U_j|>0$, $g_{i,j}=\nabla_jF_i(\theta)$, $g_j=N^{-1}\sum_i g_{i,j}$, and $\zeta_j^2=N^{-1}\sum_i\|g_{i,j}-g_j\|^2$.
\begin{lemma}[Matrix-wise selection bias]
\label{lem:fm-selection-bias}
For any updating-client set $\mathcal U_j$,
\begin{equation}
 \left\|\frac1{n_j}\sum_{i\in\mathcal U_j}g_{i,j}-g_j\right\|^2
 \le\left(\frac N{n_j}-1\right)\zeta_j^2.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
The bound relates aggregation bias to participation and gradient heterogeneity at each target matrix. Proofs are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:fm-convergence}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{Sec.conclusion}
FedMATRIX-LoRA treats individual LoRA target projections as selectable adaptation units under heterogeneous client resources. A controlled study with client-local importance scores demonstrates larger short-term adaptation gains from independent matrix selection than from whole-layer binding across the evaluated partition and checkpoint settings. The framework evaluates priorities on local data, constructs feasible configurations, and coordinates their execution under a shared budget before matrix-wise aggregation. A conditional analysis relates the resulting selective updates to effective-update bias and matrix coverage. The main benchmarks and controlled ablations assess complementary aspects of this design: the complete framework's performance and resource trade-offs, and the effects of the scoring and allocation mechanisms. Together, these results motivate importance-guided target selection as a practical dimension of resource-constrained federated adaptation.

\section{Proofs for Matrix-Wise Aggregation}
\label{app:fm-convergence}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:fm-stationarity}]
Condition on $\mathcal H_k$ and set $a=P^k\nabla F(\theta^k)$ and
$\bar v=\mathbb E[v^k\mid\mathcal H_k]=a+b^k$.
Because the update is supported on $P^k$, smoothness gives
\begin{align}
 \mathbb E[F(\theta^{k+1})\mid\mathcal H_k]
 \le{}&F(\theta^k)-\gamma\langle a,\bar v\rangle\\
 &+\frac{\beta\gamma^2}{2}
 \left(\|\bar v\|^2+\mathbb E[\|\xi^k\|^2\mid\mathcal H_k]\right).\nonumber
\end{align}
Use $2\langle a,\bar v\rangle=\|a\|^2+\|\bar v\|^2-\|b^k\|^2$ and
$\gamma\le1/\beta$ to discard the nonpositive term
$-\gamma(1-\beta\gamma)\|\bar v\|^2/2$. Taking total expectations yields
\begin{align}
 \mathbb E F(\theta^{k+1})\le{}&\mathbb E F(\theta^k)
 -\frac\gamma2\mathbb E\|P^k\nabla F(\theta^k)\|^2\\
 &+\frac\gamma2B_{\mathrm{upd}}^2
 +\frac{\beta\gamma^2}{2}S_{\mathrm{upd}}^2.\nonumber
\end{align}
Summing over $k$ telescopes the loss terms. The lower bound on $F$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac1K\sum_k\mathbb E\|P^k\nabla F(\theta^k)\|^2
 \le\frac{2D_0}{\gamma K}+B_{\mathrm{upd}}^2+\beta\gamma S_{\mathrm{upd}}^2.
\end{equation}
Apply the coverage condition and divide by $\alpha_{\mathrm{cov}}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:fm-selection-bias}]
Let $d_i=g_{i,j}-g_j$ and $w_i=\mathbf1\{i\in\mathcal U_j\}/n_j$.
Since $\sum_i d_i=0$, the selection bias equals $\sum_i(w_i-1/N)d_i$.
Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\begin{align}
 \left\|\sum_i(w_i-1/N)d_i\right\|^2
 &\le\sum_i(w_i-1/N)^2\sum_i\|d_i\|^2\\
 &=(1/n_j-1/N)N\zeta_j^2,
\end{align}
which is the claimed bound. For the full effective bias, define the remaining local-update discrepancy as $r=b-s$, with zero selection-bias blocks when no client updates a block. Then $\|b\|^2\le2\|s\|^2+2\|r\|^2$. The latter term includes local drift, optimizer effects, and any local-model mismatch; the selection lemma alone does not bound it.
\end{proof}
